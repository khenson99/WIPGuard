## Opening

Many manufacturers are told VMI is the only way. Your distributor manages it all. They monitor your inventory, decide when to replenish, and maintain control over your supply chain. For decades, this has been the gospel—especially for high-volume commodity items.

But there's another approach that's been quietly transforming shop floors across manufacturing: Kanban.

Kanban isn't new. Toyota perfected it in the 1950s and built an empire on its principles. But modern digital Kanban is different from those hand-written cards. It's faster, more visible, and puts replenishment control back where it belongs—in your hands.

This guide compares VMI and Kanban honestly. We'll show you where each excels, where each falls short, and how to know which one (or both) is right for your operation.

---

## What is VMI?

**Vendor-Managed Inventory (VMI)** is a supply chain arrangement where your supplier takes responsibility for monitoring your inventory levels and automatically triggering replenishments when stock drops below agreed thresholds.

Here's how it works in practice:

- Your distributor (or supplier) installs monitoring systems—often data feeds from your ERP or simple consumption reporting
- They track what you're using at their end of the pipe
- When inventory hits a preset minimum, they automatically ship more without waiting for your purchase order
- You pay only for what you consume, often with monthly reconciliation
- The supplier owns inventory management decisions

**VMI strengths:**
- Hands-off for the manufacturer. Once the agreement is set, replenishment happens automatically
- Can reduce manufacturer inventory holding costs (distributor holds the safety stock instead)
- Works well when consumption is highly predictable
- Reduces the administrative burden of creating purchase orders

**VMI limitations:**
- You lose visibility into your consumption patterns
- Supplier controls when and how much arrives—not always aligned with your reality
- Minimum order quantities and safety stock buffers are set by the distributor, not your actual needs
- Changing suppliers becomes costly and disruptive (switching locks you into new agreements)
- Limited flexibility if your demand suddenly shifts

## What is Kanban Replenishment?

**Kanban** comes from the Toyota Production System, where "kanban" literally means "visual signal" or "card" in Japanese. The core idea: a simple visual or digital signal tells you when to reorder something.

Modern digital Kanban works like this:

1. **Set your trigger:** You determine the reorder point based on your actual consumption and lead times (e.g., "When we have 100 units left, order 500")
2. **Signal depletion:** When inventory hits that trigger—whether via a barcode scan, digital dashboard, or physical card—an order is automatically generated
3. **Supplier delivers:** Your supplier (often the same one, but you control the terms) delivers fresh stock
4. **Manufacturer stays in control:** You set the rules, monitor the results, and adjust based on real data

**Kanban strengths:**
- You control the replenishment logic. Your thresholds, your timing, your quantities
- Complete visibility into what's being used and when
- Flexible supplier relationships—you can change terms or suppliers more easily
- Works for variable demand and multiple-supplier environments
- Encourages continuous improvement; you learn what actually drives consumption
- Low technology barrier to entry (can start with physical cards, scale to digital)

**Kanban limitations:**
- Requires more manufacturer involvement in setup and management
- Distributor doesn't assume safety stock—you do
- Demands accurate consumption data and discipline
- Needs a defined supplier or multi-supplier coordination process

## When VMI is the Better Choice

Let's be honest: VMI isn't outdated. It's the right call in specific scenarios.

**Choose VMI if:**

- **You're moving high-volume, commodity items.** If you consume 10,000 fasteners a month with zero variance, VMI's predictability engine works beautifully
- **You have a single, highly reliable supplier.** VMI thrives on simplicity and single-source dependency
- **Your team is already stretched.** If you lack the bandwidth to manage replenishment logistics, VMI's hands-off nature is valuable
- **The distributor adds real value.** Some distributors provide kitting, packaging customization, or technical support that justifies ceding control
- **Your demand is genuinely flat.** Commodity suppliers can forecast better than you can, sometimes

**Real-world VMI success:** A shop running high-volume stamping operations with massive demand for strip steel and standard fasteners can thrive under VMI. The consumption is predictable, the supplier is reliable, and the distributor's overhead is worth the automation.

## When Kanban Wins

This is where most manufacturers today are discovering competitive advantage.

**Choose Kanban if:**

- **Your demand is variable.** If you're running job-shop work, contract manufacturing, or variable product mix, Kanban's flexibility lets you respond without being locked into safety stock
- **You work with multiple suppliers.** VMI assumes one supplier controls everything. Kanban lets you source strategically from different partners, playing each against the other competitively
- **You want visibility and control.** Kanban puts replenishment data in your hands. You see what's actually being used, and you own the decisions
- **Your shop floor adoption matters.** Kanban engages operators and planners in inventory thinking. It becomes a team sport
- **You need speed to implement.** Kanban requires weeks, not months, to launch
- **You're cost-sensitive.** You pay only for what you order, no distributor overhead or safety stock premiums
- **You manage mixed MRO and production materials.** Kanban handles both without philosophical compromise

**Real-world Kanban strength:** A contract manufacturer running 15 different jobs per week with varying component pulls, supplied by 8 different vendors, can't function under VMI's rigidity. Kanban gives them agility—scan a card, reorder from whoever has the best terms that week, adjust minimums based on job flow.

## Can You Use Both?

Yes. The best manufacturers use a **hybrid approach**.

Your **VMI candidates:**
- Commodity items with flat, high-volume demand (strip steel, standard fasteners, welding wire)
- Single-source items where the distributor's value is undeniable

Your **Kanban candidates:**
- Variable-demand consumables (specialty fasteners, electrical components, custom labels)
- Multi-supplier items where you want negotiating power
- Materials where visibility and responsiveness matter operationally

**Arda works alongside existing systems.** You can maintain VMI relationships for bulk commodities while running Kanban for everything else. The two approaches don't conflict—they divide labor by use case.

This hybrid model is where we see the best results. Manufacturers get the simplicity of VMI where it fits, and the control of Kanban where it matters.

## FAQ

**Is Kanban better than VMI?**

Not universally. Kanban is better *for most manufacturers* because it returns control and visibility. But VMI excels for high-volume, predictable commodities with reliable suppliers. The right question isn't "which is better?" but "which is better for this specific material?"

**Can Kanban work for high-volume manufacturing?**

Absolutely. In fact, Kanban was born in high-volume manufacturing at Toyota. Digital Kanban scales beautifully—whether you're ordering 100 units or 100,000. The difference is that you control the thresholds, not a distributor halfway across the country.

**How do I transition from VMI to Kanban?**

Start with one category of materials—typically those with variable demand or where you're currently unhappy with service levels. Define reorder points based on 12 weeks of consumption data. Identify suppliers willing to work Kanban terms (most will). Launch with digital tracking (spreadsheet or platform). Refine thresholds monthly based on what you learn. Most shops transition completely in 2-6 months.

**What's the cost difference between VMI and Kanban?**

VMI appears free but hides costs: higher safety stock, service fees, inflated unit prices, and switching costs. Kanban costs nothing administratively, but you hold inventory instead of the distributor. Most manufacturers see 15-30% total cost reduction after switching because they're optimizing *their* consumption, not the distributor's recommendations.

**Do I need special hardware for Kanban?**

No. You can run Kanban with physical cards and a spreadsheet. That said, digital tools (barcode scanners, cloud platforms, ERP integration) make scaling easier and give you better data. Start simple, invest in tools as complexity grows.
