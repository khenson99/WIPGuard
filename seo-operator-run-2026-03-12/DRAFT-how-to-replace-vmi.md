## H1: How to Replace Vendor-Managed Inventory (Without Disrupting Production)

You signed up for vendor-managed inventory to simplify things. Fewer purchase orders. One less thing to manage. Someone else's problem.

Then reality hit.

Your distributor ran out of the one part you couldn't live without. The pricing you negotiated three years ago isn't competitive anymore. You have zero visibility into what's actually being ordered—or when. Your shop floor workers stopped trusting the system. And now you're locked into a contract that makes scaling impossible.

You've decided VMI isn't working. Now what?

This guide walks you through the practical steps to replace VMI with a system *you* control—one that manufacturers are implementing in days, not quarters.

## Signs It's Time to Replace VMI

Not every manufacturer needs to leave VMI. But if any of these hit home, it's probably time to move:

**Stockouts despite the VMI agreement.** You're hitting zero inventory on parts your distributor is supposed to keep in stock. Production stops. Your distributor blames demand forecasts. You blame them. Meanwhile, you're losing days of throughput.

**Paying more than open-market pricing.** That "convenience fee" built into your VMI contract looked reasonable in 2022. It doesn't anymore. You can buy the same parts cheaper if you had visibility and control.

**No visibility into what's being ordered or when.** Your distributor has their own system, their own forecast, their own decision-making. You find out what's in stock when you need it. That's not inventory management—that's hoping.

**Your distributor controls your production schedule.** If a key part goes short, your production plan bends to what they have available. That's backwards. Your production needs should drive inventory, not the other way around.

**You can't easily switch suppliers.** VMI contracts bundle pricing, terms, and logistics. Breaking free means renegotiating everything or eating penalties.

**Scaling means renegotiating complex contracts.** Every time you add a product line or facility, you're back at the negotiating table. VMI was supposed to scale effortlessly. It hasn't.

**Shop floor workers don't trust the system.** If your team is working around VMI—keeping their own shadow inventory, ordering safety stock offline, ignoring the "official" reorder points—the system has failed. You need what they actually need.

## Step 1: Audit Your Current VMI Setup

You can't replace VMI smart if you don't know what you're replacing. Start here.

**Document which items are under VMI.** Not all your inventory might be managed this way. Pull a list. Which SKUs are actually in the VMI agreement? Which aren't? This tells you what's in scope for replacement and what stays as-is.

**Record current costs versus market rates.** Pull your actual unit costs from the VMI contract. Then check what those same parts cost from 2-3 competing suppliers or distributors. The gap is real money you're potentially overpaying. Don't guess—confirm it.

**Track stockout frequency and impact.** How often does a critical part go short? Once a month? Once a quarter? What happens when it does—production stops, you expedite from someone else, or you've learned to keep shadow stock? Quantify the cost. This becomes your baseline for measuring whether replacement worked.

**Review contract terms and exit clauses.** Read the fine print. What's the notice period to exit? Are there penalty clauses? What data do they own versus what you own? Can you take your demand history elsewhere? These answers determine your timeline and potential friction during transition.

**Assess distributor responsiveness.** Beyond pricing, how do they perform on the other things that matter? Lead times. Order accuracy. Communication. Flexibility when you need something out of sequence. This shapes what you actually need in the replacement.

**Clarify data ownership.** You've been running through their system for years. What data do you actually own? Demand history? SKU classifications? Reorder points? You'll need this to power the replacement system.

## Step 2: Define What You Actually Need

Before you pick a new approach, get clear on requirements. This prevents you from building something that just replicates VMI's problems in a different package.

**Speed of replenishment.** VMI promised inventory on the shelf. What do you *actually* need? Same-day replenishment? Next-day? 48 hours? For which categories? Some parts might be fine on a weekly order cycle. Others need to move faster. Get specific.

**Supplier flexibility.** In VMI, you work with whoever the distributor partners with. What happens next? Do you want to work with multiple suppliers? Negotiate individual terms? Can you move volume between suppliers based on price or performance? Your new system needs to support this.

**Shop floor simplicity.** If your team has to learn complex software or run multiple systems, adoption fails. The system you build needs to be something a line leader or material handler can actually use without training manuals. Ideally: scan a card. Done.

**Visibility and control.** What do *you* need to see? Stock levels in real-time? Historical demand trends? Reorder point recommendations? Orders in-flight? What decisions do *you* need to make versus what can be automated?

**Integration with existing systems.** Does your ERP track some of this already? Does your MRP system generate material requirements? Your replacement system either needs to plug into what you have or be simple enough to stand alone.

Write these down. They're your decision criteria for step 3.

## Step 3: Choose Your Replacement Approach

Four main paths exist. Most manufacturers end up on one of these:

**Digital Kanban (like Arda).** Visual inventory signals—physical cards or a mobile app—that trigger orders when stock hits a defined level. The shop floor determines the signal. Suppliers deliver on the trigger. No forecasting. No negotiations on reorder points. Simple system. Works at scale.

**Internal purchasing automation.** You own the system. ERP generates reorder points based on consumption. Orders go out automatically. Works if your team has capacity and your ERP is flexible enough. Requires ongoing tuning. Harder to scale across multiple suppliers with different lead times.

**ERP-driven reorder points.** Similar to automation, but you set static reorder points and let the system work. Low maintenance. Works well for parts with predictable consumption. Less responsive to demand spikes or supply disruptions.

**Hybrid approach.** Kanban for fast-moving, volatile parts. ERP automation for predictable, slower-moving parts. Most balanced, but more complex to manage.

**Why most manufacturers choose Kanban:** It puts control back in the hands of the people who know what's actually happening on the floor. No forecasting. No complex contracts. No software that needs a PhD to tune. A line leader or material handler sees stock getting low, pulls a card, supplier delivers. The system adapts the moment demand changes. And because Arda was built for same-day setup, you don't spend months in implementation hell.

## Step 4: Run a Parallel Pilot

Don't flip the switch on all 500 SKUs at once. Pilot first.

**Start small.** Pick 1-2 product categories. Pick ones that matter enough that success is visible, but not so critical that a stumble stops production. If you're making engines and fasteners, maybe fasteners is your pilot. High volume. Relatively simple. Clear demand pattern.

**Run alongside VMI for 30 days.** Keep VMI running for the pilot category. Simultaneously, run your replacement system. Both feeding the same production lines. This tells you if the new system works without betting the farm.

**Measure three things:**
- **Ordering speed.** How fast can you get replenishment? From signal to delivery. Arda pilots typically see next-day or same-day delivery.
- **Stockout rate.** Fewer zeros on the shelf than you had in VMI? If yes, you've solved the core problem.
- **Cost.** Are you buying less safety stock? Negotiating better unit prices with direct supplier relationships? Seeing a real number?
- **Adoption.** Did shop floor workers actually use it? Or did they work around it? If adoption is weak, there's a training or design issue to fix before rolling out.

**Why parallel pilots work:** You get real data without real risk. Your team learns the system in a controlled environment. You build confidence before scaling.

---

## Step 5: Migrate and Expand

Once the pilot proves it works, expand systematically.

**Roll out by area, line, or material type.** Don't expand chaotically. Move from the pilot to the next logical group—another production line, another product family, another category. This keeps the change manageable and lets you apply lessons from the pilot.

**Communicate early to suppliers.** Your new suppliers need to understand the system. If you're moving from one distributor to multiple, be clear about lead times, ordering process, and expectations. Kanban-based suppliers often appreciate the visibility—they see actual demand instead of forecasts.

**Train the shop floor (briefly).** "Scan a card, place an order" takes five minutes to teach. If your training session is longer than that, the system is too complicated. Most Arda pilots need zero formal training—the concept is intuitive.

**Monitor and adjust for 30-60 days.** Stock levels right? Orders flowing on time? Any surprise bottlenecks? Make small adjustments. Reorder points too high? Lower them. Lead times longer than expected? Adjust safety stock. This is normal tuning, not failure.

## Common Mistakes When Replacing VMI

Learn from others who've done this:

**Mistake: Trying to replicate VMI's complexity.** You're replacing a system, not rebuilding it. If your new approach needs forecasting, contract negotiations, complex reorder logic, and distributor collaboration, you haven't really replaced VMI—you've just moved the problem to a different person's desk. Kanban works because it's simpler, not because it's fancier.

**Mistake: Not involving shop floor workers.** Operations managers design the system. Shop floor workers use it (or don't). If they're not part of the pilot, your system will fail adoption. Involve them from day one. Ask: "What would make your job easier?" Usually it's visibility and simplicity, not more complexity.

**Mistake: Overengineering the solution.** Don't build a 12-month ERP integration project when you could pilot Kanban in 30 days. Don't wait for perfect data or perfect forecasts. Start with what you have, learn as you go, improve from there.

**Mistake: Waiting too long to pilot.** Some manufacturers spend months evaluating, benchmarking, and planning before they run a single pilot. Meanwhile, VMI is still costing them money. Parallel pilots take 30 days. Start now.

## FAQ

**How long does it really take to replace VMI?**

A parallel pilot takes 30 days. Full migration across all categories typically takes 90-180 days, depending on your complexity. Arda customers see same-day setup for technical infrastructure; the real timeline is adoption and scaling. You move as fast as you're comfortable.

**Do I need to renegotiate with all my suppliers at once?**

No. Pilot with one or two suppliers in a pilot category. Once you've proven the model works, expand and negotiate with others. Incremental is safer than a big bang.

**What if a supplier can't meet Kanban lead times?**

That's data. If a supplier can't deliver on your needed cadence, they're not a fit. Kanban forces the truth—either they're responsive or they aren't. This is actually valuable information you didn't have under VMI.

**Can I keep some items under VMI and move others to Kanban?**

Absolutely. Hybrid approaches work. Slow-moving, predictable parts might stay on a distributor's managed program. Fast-moving, volatile parts move to Kanban where you control the signal. This is actually the most pragmatic path for many manufacturers.

**What happens to my current VMI contract?**

Read the exit clause (you pulled this in step 1). Honor the notice period. Negotiate if you can. If there's a penalty, weigh it against the ongoing cost of staying in VMI. Often the numbers make the penalty worth it.
